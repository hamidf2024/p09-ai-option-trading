 4. Define the 1% objective correctly
 There are several different possible targets: more than 1% on every trading day; an arithmetic daily
 average above 1%; a geometric daily growth rate above 1%; or more than 1% on deployed option premium.
 These are different claims. I interpret the demanding version of your goal as growth of total strategy
 account equity after trading costs, while explicitly allowing losing days.

 For an account with no external cash movements, daily return is ending net liquidation equity divided by
 beginning equity, minus one. With cash flows occurring at the measurement boundary, subtract net
 external inflows from ending equity before dividing by beginning equity. With intraday deposits or
 withdrawals, value subperiods and link their returns, or use an appropriate cash-flow-weighted
 approximation. Do not apply a simple end-of-day adjustment indiscriminately to timed intraday flows.

 Net equity includes all cash, positions, stock acquired through exercise, liabilities, financing, and accrued
 trading costs. Conservative liquidation marks and broker statement marks should both be tracked. Show
 strategy operating costs separately and also calculate an all-in result including attributable data and
 infrastructure expense. Taxes depend on the investor and should be shown separately rather than hidden in
 a generic return claim.

 5. Win rate: secondary, but the payoff distribution matters
 You are right to prioritize returns over the fraction of winning trades. A high win rate can conceal rare
 severe losses, and a low win rate can be profitable when winners sufficiently outweigh losers. But win rate
 is not irrelevant to the distribution of drawdowns, losing streaks, and capital required to survive.

 For equally sized positions measured on the same capital basis, expected trade return is the probability of
 winning times the average winning return, minus the probability of losing times the average loss
 magnitude, minus expected costs.


   Toy strategy, before costs                      Expected premium return per trade


   30% chance of +40%; 70% chance of -10%          +5.0%


   90% chance of +2%; 10% chance of -30%           -1.2%


 Neither is a forecast. The first example's assumed -10% loss is not guaranteed by placing a stop order. An
 option can gap through a stop or become difficult to sell. If 20% of account equity funded one such position,
 the stylized outcomes would be +8% or -2% of equity, with an arithmetic expectation of +1%; an
 unanticipated full premium loss would instead cost 20% of equity. Sizing from the intended stop alone
 conceals that exposure.

 Arithmetic and compounded performance also differ. A +50% day followed by a -50% day has a zero
 arithmetic average but loses 25% of starting capital. Optimize a constrained growth objective, such as
 expected log wealth, while controlling tail losses and turnover. Log-growth optimization still depends on
 uncertain forecasts and cannot manufacture an edge.

 7. Reconstructing the model and deployment workflow
 The visible modules support the following engineering reconstruction. The exact proprietary
 implementation remains unavailable.

  1. A scanner builds an eligible market universe.

  2. A feature pipeline produces model inputs.

  3. Training creates versioned checkpoints and logs gain-related metrics.

  4. Selection and ranking turn predictions into candidate trades.

  5. Tradability or eligibility filters and portfolio controls constrain entries.

  6. Broker-specific workers route orders and manage positions.

  7. An independent exit scheduler and reconciliation jobs maintain state.

  8. Dashboards expose health, positions, opening-date outcomes, and checkpoint metadata.

 The model checkpoint in A12 strengthens the connection between training artifacts and the trading
 dashboard. It does not expose the artifact or prove that every displayed PnL table used the same weights.
 The deployment name m14.fund3 and training name m14.L.fund3 should be linked through an explicit model
 registry rather than assumed identical from their spelling.

 13. Training and validation protocol
 Use chronological, nested evaluation. Randomly splitting option rows lets related contracts, repeated
 observations, and overlapping outcomes leak information across train and test sets. Thousands of options
 observed on the same day do not constitute thousands of independent market histories.

 forward through subsequent years. At each step, learn preprocessing, tune parameters, and choose models

 14. Execution simulation: where a paper edge often
 disappears
 Build an event-driven simulator around what could have been submitted and filled. For a deliberately
 conservative long-option baseline, buy at a valid post-decision ask and sell at a valid post-exit-decision bid,
 with additional latency, capacity limits, and actual fees. That is a baseline assumption, not a guarantee of
 those fills. Market movement between decision, submission, and exchange acknowledgment can make
 execution worse.

 Limit orders can improve price but can also remain unfilled. A midpoint appearing on a quote screen does
 not imply your order would execute there. Official options education describes the price-versus-fill tradeoff
 and the importance of available bid/ask liquidity. [22]

 Illustrative arithmetic: an option is quoted $1.95/$2.05, midpoint $2.00. Later it is $2.05/$2.15, midpoint
 $2.10. A midpoint-only backtest records a 5% gain. Buying at the initial ask and selling at the later bid
 produces zero gross gain; fees turn it into a loss. With an unchanged $1.95/$2.05 quote, immediately
 crossing both sides loses $10 per standard contract before fees, or about 4.88% of the $205 purchase cost.

 Required handling includes quote staleness and crossed markets; order size relative to accessible liquidity;
 partial fills; cancel/replace races; rejected orders; outages; transaction fees; early closes; and the ordering
 of events within bars. If a bar hits both a stop and a profit target, its high and low alone cannot tell which
 happened first. Use finer data or a conservative unresolved-case rule.

 For spreads, simulate a supported complex order or conservative leg handling. Do not independently
 assume every leg fills at an advantageous midpoint. Reject a strategy if most of its profit depends on
 unverifiable favorable fill assumptions.

 Alpaca explicitly says its paper environment omits factors including market impact, latency slippage, queue
 position, and regulatory fees. Therefore, an Alpaca-labeled PnL column cannot by itself establish live
 achievable performance. Paper mode is valuable for workflow and reconciliation checks; a later controlled
 live pilot is needed to measure actual fills if the strategy first passes research review. [16]



 15. Portfolio sizing and the target's risk implications
 Keep forecast generation separate from position sizing. Rank candidates by expected net dollar
 contribution, downside, and incremental portfolio exposure, rather than the largest predicted percentage
 on the cheapest option. Size only after accounting for already-open and pending orders.

 For long options, full premium plus costs is the relevant contractual loss before any exercise-related stock
 exposure. For debit spreads, use the properly constructed defined-risk loss and account for assignment,
 exercise, and legging complications. An intended stop does not turn an option into a guaranteed
 smaller-loss instrument. OCC explains the possibility of losing the entire premium and the obligations
 associated with exercise and assignment. [19, 20]
