Yes. I treated this as a deep research question specifically for ML applied to options/derivatives trading, rather than simply asking “which neural network predicts stocks best.”

The most important finding is this:

The best ML model for options is usually not the model with the lowest prediction error.
The best model is the one that produces a stable, out-of-sample economic edge after transaction costs, bid/ask spreads, slippage, volatility-regime changes, tail events and realistic execution.

That distinction is strongly supported by recent options research: models can improve pricing RMSE substantially while failing to improve actual trading P&L. A 2026 study explicitly demonstrates this disconnect between option-pricing accuracy and realized trading performance.
SSRN

The critical distinction: prediction ≠ profitability

This is one of the strongest conclusions from the research.

A model can have:

excellent RMSE

but:

terrible trading returns.

Why?

Because option prices contain:

bid/ask spread,

volatility risk premium,
skew,

transaction costs,
dealer inventory,
early-exercise effects,
discrete hedging costs.

A 2026 study using 18.2 million minute-bar S&P 500 call observations explicitly compares model pricing error with realized P&L from a mechanical trading rule. The authors emphasize that lower pricing error does not necessarily translate into better trading performance.

🥇 My #1 choice for most practical options ML

If you gave me a serious options dataset and said:

“Build the first profitable ML model.”

I would probably start with XGBoost.

Tree boosting is exceptionally good at nonlinear interactions between these variables.

For example:

IV may be expensive only when VIX is high and skew is steep and realized volatility is falling.

A linear model struggles with this.

XGBoost can naturally learn it.

Evidence

An option-pricing study found that machine-learning methods outperform traditional Black-Scholes/Corrado-Su approaches, with additive boosting models showing the best prediction performance.
ScienceDirect

Another study comparing nonparametric option-pricing models found Extreme Gradient Boosting and Random Forest among the most accurate approaches across market regimes.
PubMed Central (PMC)

Research specifically on implied-volatility forecasting also evaluates XGBoost against neural networks and classical models, highlighting the importance of feature engineering and leakage control.

Weakness

3. Transformer / Autoformer
🥉 My highest-potential model for a large research operation

Transformers are particularly interesting if you have:

many stocks,
many options,
long histories,
macro data,
news,
fundamentals,
volatility surfaces,
cross-asset data.

A Transformer can learn relationships across long sequences and heterogeneous information.

Strong recent evidence

A 2025/26 study compared Autoformer with simpler neural networks using:

all U.S. stocks,
920 features,
data going back to 1957,
1-, 3-, and 12-month horizons.

Autoformer consistently achieved lower out-of-sample MSE than the simpler neural networks, with especially strong improvements at longer horizons.

The important point is not:

“Transformers always beat everything.”

They don't.

When you have large, heterogeneous datasets and long temporal relationships, Transformers become much more attractive.

4. LightGBM

LightGBM is extremely attractive for options because option datasets can become enormous.

Imagine:

10 years
100 strikes
20 expirations
calls + puts
daily observations.

You quickly get millions/billions of rows.

LightGBM is designed for efficient gradient boosting on large datasets.

Option-specific evidence

A 2026 NIFTY50 option-pricing study combines:

VAE → LightGBM → Monte Carlo

where LightGBM predicts implied volatility using market and investor-sentiment variables.

The integrated framework reportedly improves option-pricing accuracy relative to Black-Scholes across moneyness and maturity segments.
ScienceDirect
+1

This is an especially interesting architecture because it shows how ML can be combined with classical derivative pricing rather than simply replacing it.

Its relevance to options is unusually strong.

A Journal of Banking & Finance paper developed GRUδ, a sequential option-hedging model using S&P 500 European option-market data.

It reported that weekly and monthly hedging performance significantly surpassed several competing data-driven and classical approaches.

Random Forest is one of the most useful models to have in your research stack because it is:

9. Graph Neural Networks

This is one of the most exciting models specifically for options.

Why?

Because options aren't independent.

Consider:

SPX 5000 call, 30D

SPX 5050 call, 30D

SPX 5100 call, 30D

SPX 5000 put, 30D

SPX 5000 call, 60D

These contracts are structurally related.

Traditional models often treat each option independently.

A GNN can represent the entire option chain as a graph.

Evidence

A Journal of Futures Markets study created a deep GNN incorporating momentum spillover effects among related options using SSE 50 ETF options.

The model improved RMSE by at least 8.81% relative to classical models.
Wiley Online Library
+1

A new 2026 study goes even further, representing the option chain as a multi-relational graph where contracts connect through:

expiry,
strike,
option type,
time-series relationships.
SSRN
My view

GNN is probably one of the most promising next-generation option-specific architectures.

10. PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization

This is fundamentally different.

Instead of:

predict tomorrow's volatility

you ask:

what should the trading agent do?

Possible actions:

buy call,
sell call,
buy put,
sell put,
hold,
hedge,
roll,
change position size.

PPO is attractive because it handles sequential decision-making.

Option evidence

Recent research compares eight deep-RL algorithms for dynamic option hedging.

The study found:

Monte Carlo Policy Gradient

followed by:

PPO

performed best according to its root semi-quadratic penalty metric, while Monte Carlo Policy Gradient was the only method to outperform the Black-Scholes delta hedge under its computational budget.
arXiv

Another 2026 study uses risk-aware RL with transaction costs and finds that a learned policy improved risk-adjusted performance relative to no-hedge, momentum and volatility-targeting baselines, although the authors explicitly stop short of claiming formal dominance.
arXiv

That caution is important.

2. Leakage — potentially the #1 reason fake ML strategies work

This is critical.

Suppose you predict tomorrow's return using today's:

closing IV

Fine.

But if your dataset accidentally contains:

tomorrow's closing IV

you have a completely fraudulent model.

Options data has especially complicated leakage problems because:

contracts disappear,
expirations change,
IV is derived from prices,
bid/ask updates asynchronously,

The biggest lesson from the research

The research does not say:

“Deep learning beats traditional ML.”

It says something much more nuanced.

Trees

Excellent for nonlinear tabular data.

LSTM/GRU

Excellent when temporal volatility dynamics matter.

Transformers

Potentially powerful with large heterogeneous datasets and long dependencies.

GNNs

Potentially exceptional for the structure of option chains.

RL

Potentially useful for sequential decisions and hedging, but much harder to validate.

Simple regularized models

Still extremely important benchmarks.

And the strongest evidence says:

The model's economic objective, data, validation and execution can matter more than the architecture itself.

The Office of Financial Research's new 2026 open benchmark makes essentially this broader point: no forecasting method is universally best across financial data-generating processes, so models need to be compared on the same data and forecasting task.
